01

The short version

  • The house: a ranch with a basement. Oil boiler, a 2013 4-ton central AC with its air handler and ducts in the attic, solar panels, and a big insulation job in April 2026 (attic from about R-7 to R-59.5, empty walls dense-packed).
  • The plan: replace the AC and its attic air handler with a 3-ton cold-climate ducted heat pump on the existing ducts. It becomes the main heat. The oil boiler stays for the basement and as backup.
  • Why 3 tons: the house loses about 28k BTU/h on a 5°F night, and the unit makes 39k there. Bigger units would cycle on and off most of the winter.
  • Why DIY: Connecticut lets homeowners permit work on their own house, this brand's warranty survives a homeowner install, and my dad handles the refrigerant. About $8k, against $17.5k net for the best contractor quote.
  • Is it worth it: +$14.6k over 25 years against staying on oil. It stays positive in every single-input sensitivity, and the worst combination I could build is still +$3.8k.

03

The oil bills knew the answer

Every sizing method starts with one number: how much heat the house loses on a cold night. The usual way to get it is a Manual J, a room-by-room calculation from wall areas, R-values and assumed air leakage. I had something better: last winter's oil deliveries. Each fill-up says how many gallons the house burned since the last one, and hourly weather from Bradley airport says how cold it was in between.

From slope to heat loss: 0.145 gallons per degree-day × 138,500 BTU per gallon × ~80% boiler efficiency ÷ 24 hours ≈ 670 BTU/h for every degree of difference between inside and out, for the whole house last winter. Then three adjustments, each with its uncertainty carried through a 200,000-draw Monte Carlo:

  • The insulation job cut the main floor's heat loss by about 39% (30–47%). Cross-check: air conditioning energy per cooling degree-day fell 28% the following summer, measured from the solar system's 15-minute data.
  • Only the main floor counts. The basement stays on the boiler. Its zone never ran last winter, so the basement floated on heat leaking down through an R-19 ceiling.
  • Attic ducts lose 5–25% of the heat on the way (12% median).

Result: 27.8k BTU/h at 5°F (p10–p90: 23.6–32.4k). 5°F is the 99.6% design temperature from 15 winters of hourly weather.

What could make the oil number read low? Hidden electric heat (there wasn't any: electric use barely rose on cold days), a missing delivery (the ledger reconciles), or a room zone left off all winter. That last one is the open question, and it's why the backup plan matters.

The house, room by room

The permit's Manual J needed every wall, window and ceiling in the house, so the main floor already exists as data: 13 rooms, 29 walls, 28 windows by size and direction, 4 skylights and three kinds of ceiling. That makes it possible to see where the heat actually goes, and to run last winter's weather through it.

05

Bigger isn't better: the minimum matters more than the maximum

Contractors size for the coldest night, but a Connecticut winter spends very little time there: about 29 hours a year below 5°F, and half the heating season above 38°F. An inverter heat pump can only turn down so far. When the house needs less than the unit's minimum output, it cycles on and off, which costs efficiency and comfort.

Cooling agrees. On the hottest day of summer 2026 (99°F), the old 4-ton AC still sat idle about half the afternoon. The measured steady peak is 21–23k, and the 3-ton makes 42k at 95°F. A smaller unit runs longer at low speed, and long slow runs are what pull humidity out of the air.

Why MrCool

The warranty. Most brands void coverage unless a licensed contractor installs the unit. MrCool's stays at 10 years on parts, compressor and labor if you use its pre-charged quick-connect line set, which needs no brazing and no vacuum pump. The contractor's quote was for the Innovair version of this exact unit.

The trap: the same MrCool air handler is also sold with a weaker outdoor unit that makes 27k at 5°F instead of 39k, and the listings look nearly identical. Whatever the brand, check the AHRI certificate number of the matched pair before you buy. Ours is 216032532.

07

Keeping the boiler, and why the strips stay off

The oil boiler stays, for two reasons:

  • The basement. Its ceiling has R-19 insulation, so it can't borrow much heat from upstairs. Without its own zone it would drop to roughly 35–45°F in a cold snap. Removing the boiler means adding a basement heater and a ~41k whole-house load, more than the 3-ton makes at 5°F.
  • Backup. The boiler's main-floor zones sit a few degrees under the heat pump's setpoint (64–65°F against 68°F), so they catch any shortfall automatically.

That's why the 10 kW of electric strips are wired as emergency heat only. On a normal two-stage thermostat they'd come on during every morning recovery, at three times the heat pump's cost, before the boiler ever fired. The rest of the control plan:

  • Small setbacks (2°F at most). Big recoveries are where any heat pump runs flat out.
  • Start on single-stage calls and watch the cycling. A 2024 ACEEE field study found the same unit ran at a COP of 2.0 when its controls made it cycle and 3.4 once they let it modulate.
  • Pellet inserts in the fireplaces? Checked: they lose money (−$8.4k over 25 years), because pellet heat costs about what oil does.

The solar wrinkle. The house has solar panels on net metering and makes more than it uses, so the surplus piles up as bill credits that are hard to cash out. The heat pump soaks that surplus up: with it running, the house becomes a small net importer (about 830 kWh a year), so the credits get spent at full value instead of stranding. How much the banked credits would otherwise be worth is the biggest unknown in the model, which is why it tops the chart below.

09

Wiring, permits and code: by the book

First, a licensed electrician replaced the old 100 A service with a 200 A panel, with spaces reserved for the heat pump. It needed doing anyway; even charged to this project, the NPV stays positive (+$8.6k). The heat pump then needs three circuits:

CircuitBreaker / wireWhy
Outdoor unit30 A 2-pole on the old AC's 10 AWG runMCA 29 A / max 30 A. Connecticut exempts inverter HVAC from the outdoor GFCI rule
10 kW heat strips60 A 2-pole, 6 AWG copper to the atticMCA 53 A. Emergency heat only
Air handler blower15 A 2-pole, its own feedHas to stay on: it powers the refrigerant leak sensor (R-454B is mildly flammable)

Permits. Connecticut lets homeowners pull electrical and mechanical permits for work on a house they own and live in. The town wanted a Manual J with the mechanical application, so I ran one myself on CoolCalc ($45, ACCA-approved): compliant, with the 3-ton at 111% of the cooling load. The 2022 Connecticut code governs (the 2026 code was rejected in April 2026). What an attic air handler needs to pass:

  • A secondary drain pan with a float switch that shuts the unit off, and a trapped primary drain.
  • A light switched at the hatch, a receptacle, a 24 in walkway and a 30 × 30 in service platform above the insulation.
  • A receptacle within 25 ft of the outdoor unit.
  • CO alarms on every level, basement included. The electrical permit triggers that in a house with an oil boiler.
  • No duct leakage test for an equipment swap on existing ducts; seal the new connections with UL 181 mastic. Bedroom transfer grilles go through walls, not as new jump ducts through the attic.
  • Refrigerant: my dad holds an EPA 608 Universal certification. He recovers the old R-410A (nothing gets vented) and handles the refrigerant side; the new system arrives pre-charged.

10

Seven loggers and a solar monitor

Since late September, seven temperature and humidity loggers have been recording the rooms that matter, alongside the solar system's 15-minute electricity data. A few things they've already shown:

  • Humidity runs above 60% in every room. The house is tight now and holds household moisture.
  • The basement held 64–65°F with no heat in early October, 1–3°F warmer than my model predicts. Good news for the winter.
  • The bedroom wing runs 1–4°F cooler than the hall thermostat, so the ducts need balancing toward it.
  • No sign of warm house air leaking into the attic at the whole-house fan cover.

After the install, the scorecard: this winter's heat pump kWh against the predicted 4,700–4,900, how often it cycles, and the basement's oil use. Next spring that data decides whether the boiler stays or a basement mini-split replaces it.

11

If you're shopping for mini-splits

Most of this carries over from ducted to ductless.

  1. Size from your bills, not a rule of thumb.

    A year of oil, gas or propane deliveries plus free hourly weather data gives you the real heat loss. Ours came in about a third below the room-by-room estimate and well below two Manual Js. Ask any contractor to show the number their size is based on.

  2. Read two numbers on the NEEP list.

    Max output at 5°F keeps you warm. Min output at 47°F decides how much it cycles. NEEP's cold-climate list (ashp.neep.org) has both for every certified unit.

  3. Small rooms are the hard part.

    Our bedrooms need 1–2k on a design night, and the smallest wall heads can't turn down below about 2.4k, so a head in every bedroom would cycle all winter. One unit covering a group of small rooms does better.

  4. Check the 115V spec, not the brochure.

    Many of the best cold-climate lines only come in 230V. If a 115V model is on the table, look up that exact model's 5°F output.

    230V isn't harder to wire: same cable, half the current; it just takes two breaker spaces. If the panel is short on space, ask about tandem breakers or a small subpanel.

  5. Rebates come with strings.

    Connecticut pays $1,500 per ton, only through approved contractors. Massachusetts' Mass Save pays $1,125 per ton for partial-home systems in 2026 (up to $8,500) and $2,650 per ton whole-home, with an installer from its Heat Pump Installer Network and a unit on its qualified list. Price the job both ways.

  6. Know your state's rules.

    Connecticut lets homeowners permit their own electrical and mechanical work. In Massachusetts it depends on the town, and many only issue electrical permits to licensed electricians, which also keeps the paperwork clean for a future sale. The state publishes a mini-split permitting guide.

  7. Warranty terms decide DIY.

    Most brands void the warranty on a homeowner install. If you're doing any of it yourself, get the brand's DIY terms in writing first.

  8. Controls matter.

    Keep setbacks small and let the unit run low and slow. The same hardware can deliver about 70% more heat per kWh when it modulates instead of cycling.

12

How this was done

Data

  • Oil: the delivery ledger, seven fill-to-fill intervals, 840 gallons (Sep 2025 to Apr 2026).
  • Weather: hourly temperatures at Bradley International Airport, 2011–2026: 15 heating seasons.
  • Electricity: 15-minute consumption and production from the solar monitor, Oct 2025 to Sep 2026, plus 2025 utility bills.
  • Equipment: NEEP's cold-climate heat pump list (min and max capacity and efficiency at 47, 17 and 5°F), checked against ENERGY STAR and AHRI certificates.
  • House: a blower-door test (4,397 CFM50, about 7.4 ACH50, before the insulation job), the insulation invoice, and a floor plan and window takeoff from 3D scans.
  • Rooms: seven temperature and humidity loggers since Sep 30, 2026.

Models

  • A Monte Carlo rebuild of the heat loss (200,000 draws) from the oil regression, insulation, duct and basement assumptions.
  • An hour-by-hour simulation of each candidate size over 15 winters, with cycling penalties and a defrost derate.
  • A 25-year cash-flow model with replacements, net metering and one-at-a-time sensitivities.
  • A second, skeptical pass over all of it on Oct 1. It moved the budget from $6.0k to $8.0k and the NPV from +$20.7k to +$14.6k, and changed none of the decisions.
