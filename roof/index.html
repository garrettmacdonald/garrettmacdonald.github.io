How insurance paid for our whole roof

A windstorm damaged one side of our roof in central Connecticut. The insurer's first offer was to fix that side, about 23 of the roof's 40 squares. Six months later we had a new roof on every slope, plus our 40 solar panels taken down and put back, and we paid only the $1,500 deductible. Connecticut's matching law is a big reason why. Here's how it went and how you can do the same.

01

The short version

If your roof has real storm damage and your shingles can't be matched anymore, Connecticut law can turn a one-slope repair into a full roof replacement.

  • It started with a free inspection. A roofer left a flier offering free ice-dam inspections. Their rep found wind damage, a leaking stove vent, leaks around the chimney and a moldy skylight.
  • We filed a windstorm claim and had the roofer at the adjuster's inspection. The adjuster agreed the damage was covered, but only wrote up the damaged side, a bit over half the roof.
  • The roofer pulled a shingle for a match test. In Connecticut, if new shingles won't match the old ones next to them, the insurer has to replace enough of the roof that it looks uniform. With an older roof, that usually means all of it.
  • Two weeks later the insurer revised to the whole roof, about 42 squares. A later supplement added $13,000 to take down and reinstall our solar panels, which we had assumed wouldn't be covered.
  • We paid the $1,500 deductible and nothing else for the roof. We did choose to spend our own money on new skylights while the roof was open.

02

The Connecticut matching law

Most homeowners' policies promise to repair damage with material of "like kind and quality." Connecticut goes further and writes a matching requirement into state law.

The statute is Connecticut General Statutes §38a-316e, passed in 2013 (Public Act 13-138). In plain English:

If a covered loss means replacing items that won't match the adjacent items in quality, color or size, the insurer has to replace all of those items with like kind and quality material, so the result has a reasonably uniform appearance. It applies inside and outside the house. My paraphrase of Conn. Gen. Stat. §38a-316e(a)

What that means for a roof

Each face of a roof is a "slope" (also called a plane or elevation). Storms usually damage one or two slopes, and the insurer's first estimate usually covers only those. The roof is one continuous surface, though, so every slope that touches the damaged one is an adjacent item. If the new shingles on the repaired slope won't match the old shingles on the slopes around it, the law requires the insurer to replace those slopes too. On a typical house, that ends up being the whole roof.

Why discontinued shingles matter

Shingle lines and colors change every few years, and a roof that's 15 or 20 years old has also faded. If the exact shingle isn't made anymore, a new one can't match in color, size or profile. Then the one-slope repair can't produce a "reasonably uniform appearance," and the matching rule applies.

The standard way to settle this is an ITEL report. The roofer (or adjuster) cuts a shingle sample and sends it to ITEL, a lab insurers use. ITEL identifies the shingle, says whether it's still made, and names the closest match currently available. Our first adjuster suggested this in writing: if the shingles were still manufactured, the contractor could match the old and new sections. That is the opening. If they aren't, the partial estimate doesn't hold up. Our roofer cut the sample on April 15, and on April 27 the insurer revised to the whole roof. Nothing in the paperwork says what the test found, but by the adjuster's own rule, that is the result you'd get if the shingle couldn't be matched.

If the insurer won't budge: appraisal

Most homeowners' policies have an appraisal clause. Each side picks an appraiser, and an umpire breaks ties. In 2022 the Connecticut Supreme Court held in Klass v. Liberty Mutual that once an insurer admits the damage is covered, how much undamaged material it must replace to get a uniform appearance is part of the "amount of loss." That makes it a question for appraisal, decided case by case, rather than a lawsuit. That case looked a lot like ours: a few shingles blew off the back of the roof, the insurer offered to replace only the rear slopes, and the homeowner wanted the whole roof. We didn't need appraisal, but it's good to know it's there.

What the law does not do

  • It doesn't create coverage. There has to be real damage from a covered peril, like wind or hail. Old age and wear aren't covered.
  • "Adjacent" has limits. In a 2019 siding case, a Connecticut court read "adjacent" narrowly and didn't make the insurer replace siding on walls that didn't touch the damaged area. On a roof, the slopes all join at ridges and hips, which is why the argument works so well there.
  • Your policy still matters. Some policies pay roofs at actual cash value (depreciated) instead of replacement cost, or exclude "cosmetic" hail damage. Check your declarations page.

04

Where the money came from

The final estimate came to $43,323. Insurance paid $41,823 of it, and the $1,500 difference was our deductible.

Why it arrived in four checks

Insurers pay replacement-cost claims in two stages. First they pay the actual cash value: the cost of the new roof minus depreciation for the old roof's age. They hold back the depreciation (about $12,000 for us) until the work is done and you send the final invoice. Code-upgrade items and the permit are paid when the work is done, too. So the early checks look small. That's normal, and you get the rest.

05

How to do this yourself

  1. Get a free inspection

    Have a reputable local roofer that does insurance work look at the roof, especially after a big wind or hail storm. Ask them to photograph any damage: lifted, creased or missing shingles, and hail bruising.

  2. File promptly, for the right peril

    If there's real storm damage, call your insurer and file it as wind or hail damage with a date. Policies have deadlines for reporting and for disputing a claim, so don't sit on it.

  3. Have the roofer at the inspection

    The adjuster may spend 20 minutes on your roof. A roofer who does insurance work will point out every damaged spot and get on the roof with them. We signed a short authorization so ours could talk to the insurer directly.

  4. Expect a partial first offer

    A one-slope estimate isn't a no. Read it: if it covers the damage but not the rest of the roof, that's the setup for the matching argument.

  5. Ask for an ITEL test

    Have the roofer cut a sample and send it in, or ask the adjuster to. If the shingle is discontinued or the closest match won't blend, ask in writing for the adjacent slopes under §38a-316e.

  6. Get your solar quote early

    If you have panels, get a written detach-and-reset quote from your installer and send it to the adjuster. Taking the panels off is part of fixing the roof. Ours was $13,000 and fully covered.

  7. Sign a contract that matches the payout

    Look for a roofer whose contract price is the insurance estimate, including any supplements, so you owe the deductible and nothing else. Read the fine print on late fees and what counts as "complete."

  8. Collect the held-back money

    When the roof is done, send the final invoice and photos to the insurer. That releases the depreciation and the code and permit items. It's real money: $15,280 for us.

06

Things to watch out for

  • The damage has to be real. This only works for genuine storm damage. Exaggerating a claim is insurance fraud. A roofer who offers to "take care of" your deductible is a red flag.
  • Insurance checks can be made out to you and your mortgage company. Ours were made out to just us, but if you have a mortgage, the lender may need to endorse them.
  • Don't pay the last dollar until the job is finished. Our roofer left a pallet, leftover materials and nails in the yard after the install. We waited until a magnet sweep and the pickup were done before paying the final balance.
  • Know your contract's late-fee clauses. Ours charged 1.5% a month on late balances and allowed a 35% collections fee after 30 days. You're usually supposed to pass insurance money through within 30 days of getting it.
  • Checks get lost. Our final check was mailed and returned to the insurer for no clear reason. They stopped payment and reissued it electronically within a week.
  • Extras aren't covered. Skylights, gutters and upgrades are out of pocket unless they were damaged too. That said, the roof is open anyway, so it's the cheapest time to do them.
  • The permit closes after you pay. The roofer pulls the permit and schedules the final inspection, and many won't close it until they're paid. Make sure it actually gets closed.
