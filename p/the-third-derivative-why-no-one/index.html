The Third Derivative: Why No One Understands "The Second Derivative"

The internet misremembers calculus. The same mistake runs through the summer's most-shared AI-bear essay.

Slopbreaker
Oct 28, 2026
232

Ask a Substack author what caused the 2008 mortgage crisis and you will get a neat little story: home prices stopped rising faster, a hidden variable nobody was watching rolled over, the refinancing treadmill stalled, and the defaults followed. Acceleration fell, therefore borrowers defaulted. Its chief virtue is that it sounds clever. Its chief defect is that the sequence is backwards. And it is the same mistake a much-shared essay called "The Second Derivative" is now making about the AI boom.

The subprime machine did not run on the second derivative. It ran on money, and more precisely on the price of money. Between June 2004 and June 2006 the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate seventeen consecutive times, from 1% to 5.25%. The canonical product of the era - the 2/28 hybrid adjustable-rate mortgage - reset off six-month LIBOR plus a margin of five or six points. A borrower who signed in 2004, with LIBOR under 2%, met a reset in 2006 with LIBOR above 5%. That is not a change in a change. That is a payment shock, and a payment shock is a level.

Look at the timing. The loans that went bad first were not the seasoned 2003 vintage struggling to refinance into slowing appreciation. They were the 2006 vintage - loans that defaulted within months of origination, before any reset, before any refinance was attempted, before the second derivative of anything could have reached them. Yuliya Demyanyk and Otto Van Hemert documented it in the Review of Financial Studies: loan quality deteriorated for six consecutive years before the crisis, and rising prices hid it. Early-payment default is not a calculus problem. It is an underwriting problem. A borrower who misses the third payment on a no-documentation loan did not consult the Case-Shiller index first.

The essay's own opening chart is the tell. It plots home-price appreciation and labels the line, in its own legend, as the first derivative. Underneath, in small gray type, the footnote concedes that the series are representative and "stylized to show timing, not tick-level values." The figure built to prove that defaults ran on the second derivative plots the first derivative, from data drawn to show the timing it was drawn to show. That is not evidence. That is an illustration of a conclusion.

And then there is the part the essay leaves out entirely, which is the only part that made 2008 into 2008. Subprime mortgages outstanding were roughly $1.3 trillion in early 2007. The dot-com crash erased something like $5 trillion of equity value. The smaller loss produced the far larger crisis, and the reason has nothing to do with derivatives of home prices. Subprime paper sat inside broker-dealers levered thirty to one, funded overnight in the repo market and in asset-backed commercial paper. When the collateral became suspect, the lenders' lenders ran. Gary Gorton and Andrew Metrick gave it its proper name: a run on repo. 2008 was not a deceleration. It was a bank run without a bank.

Search the essay. It mentions the Federal Reserve zero times. LIBOR, zero. Interest rates, zero. Repo, zero. Maturity, zero. Deposits, zero. A 6,800-word essay about the lessons of 2008 that never names the price of money or the run is not a theory of 2008. It is a mood with Roman numerals.

II. A Shorter Theory of Derivatives

Call the thing being measured S. Its level is how big it is. Its first derivative is how fast it is growing. Its second derivative is whether that growth is speeding up or slowing down. So far, so ninth grade.

The essay's central claim is that markets track the first two closely and barely notice the third. But a negative second derivative has a common English name. It is called growth slowed. It is, by a comfortable margin, the most-watched quantity in finance. Every earnings preview on Wall Street is an argument about acceleration and deceleration. "Decel" is the sell-side's favorite word. An entire independent research firm, Hedgeye, built its brand on "rate of change" and markets itself on trading the second derivative of growth and inflation. Macro desks track the credit impulse - the change in the flow of new credit, which is literally the second derivative of the debt stock - and have since Michael Biggs, Thomas Mayer and Andreas Pick popularized it in 2010. Purchasing-managers indexes, among the most closely watched releases in macro, are diffusion indexes: they measure whether conditions are improving faster or slower. The claim that almost no one trades on the second derivative is false in a way that takes one search to discover.

The deeper problem is what the second derivative actually does over the life of anything that succeeds. Every technology that has ever been adopted at scale traced an S-curve. On that curve the second derivative turns negative at the inflection point - the exact midpoint of adoption - and stays negative for the entire second half of the curve, while the level keeps rising for years or decades. Electricity, the automobile, the telephone, the smartphone: each spent most of its commercial life with a negative second derivative. If a negative second derivative were a death sentence, every successful technology in history would have died at fifty percent penetration. Anthropic's new S-1 says it in plain English in its risk factors: "We expect our revenue growth rate to decline over time." That is a negative second derivative, disclosed in an SEC filing - the same filing that reports a quarter of operating profit.

The essay's most quotable idea is "borrowed time": the window between the moment the second derivative rolls over and the moment the first derivative crosses zero, during which everything looks fine and the machine is already broken. On an S-curve, that window is infinite. The growth rate approaches zero and never crosses it. In the essay's vocabulary, borrowed time is simply time.

Try it on the essay's own example. Suppose home prices had risen exactly 6% a year, every year, forever. The first derivative is constant and the second derivative is zero. Every 2/28 borrower builds equity on schedule and refinances on schedule, indefinitely; the treadmill runs with no acceleration at all. Now suppose prices rise 20%, then 15%, then 10%, then 8%. The second derivative is negative every single year, and a borrower who bought at the start is sitting on a mountain of equity and can refinance whenever she likes. What breaks a 2/28 is not a negative second derivative. It is the level of the borrower's equity at the reset - how far prices have risen since her purchase, against how much she borrowed - and the level of the rate she resets to. The borrowers who failed were the ones who bought late, at the highest prices, with nothing down, into a reset off a far higher LIBOR. Every one of those is a level.

None of this means slowing growth is harmless. It means slowing growth is a fact about every growth story, not a hidden variable, and that the useful question - the one the essay never asks - is whether some specific obligation requires a specific growth rate to be serviced. That is a question about a borrower, a contract and a balance sheet. It is answered with levels.

The essay is right about one thing in this section: a fixed-coupon credit instrument is negatively convex, and equity is not. But negative convexity hurts the holder only if the borrower actually cannot pay, and whether the borrower can pay depends on the borrower. A fifteen-year data-center lease to Microsoft is negatively convex in exactly the way a Microsoft bond is negatively convex. Nobody loses sleep over Microsoft bonds.

If we are going to rank variables by how few people watch them, the second derivative loses to the third. In physics the third derivative of position is called jerk. The fourth is called snap, the fifth crackle, the sixth pop. I mention this only so the next essay in the series has a title.

III. Fine. Call It Real Estate.

The essay's boldest move is a reclassification. AI, it says, is not a technology cycle at all. It is a credit-driven real estate cycle wearing a hoodie: data centers on entitled land, debt against the structure, take-or-pay tenants, long construction lags that guarantee supply arrives after demand has turned. In the essay's telling, the servers are incidental; the business is property.

Grant the frame. If this is real estate, then we should measure it with real estate's instruments - vacancy, preleasing, speculative construction - and the essay, which spends a full section insisting on the real estate analogy, never does. CBRE does. Its North America data center report for the first half of 2026 puts vacancy at 1.4%, a record low, down from 1.6% a year earlier. Construction is also at a record, 7,481 megawatts across the primary markets. And 80.4% of that capacity under construction is already preleased, up from 74.3% a year earlier. In Northern Virginia, the largest data center market on earth, vacancy is 0.24%. CBRE estimates the unleased capacity still under construction covers roughly six months of demand.

Real estate cycles break when speculative supply arrives into rising vacancy. This market has the lowest vacancy ever recorded, and it builds four-fifths of its new supply only after a tenant has signed. You can call that many things. A glut-in-waiting is not one of them.

Nor do real estate cycles break the same way every time, on a slowdown in demand growth against new supply, as the essay asserts. The best-studied American property busts had more mundane triggers. The REIT collapse of 1973-75 hit construction lenders that had funded themselves with short-term bank loans and commercial paper just as short-term rates spiked. The commercial real estate crash of the late 1980s followed the Tax Reform Act of 1986, which removed the tax shelters that had justified much of the building, and it was financed in large part by savings and loans lending insured deposits into speculative projects. Rates, tax law, deposit funding. The common thread is how the buildings were financed and what that money cost - levels, again.

Then there is the history. The essay's entire 2000-versus-2008 argument rests on a single sentence of fact: that the dot-com bust was an equity event, with thin balance sheets and almost no debt, and therefore offers no precedent for a debt-financed build-out. That is half of 2000. The other half was the telecom and fiber boom - the most debt-financed technology build-out of the twentieth century, and the closest precedent we have to everything the essay describes. Long-lived hard assets. Long-dated capacity contracts, sold as indefeasible rights of use. Vendor financing from equipment makers to their own customers. A handful of heavily levered builders. Capacity swaps booked as revenue - backlog that wasn't.

When it broke, it broke in the credit market. Edward Altman and Gaurav Bana at NYU found that telecom accounted for roughly 57% of high-yield default dollars through the third quarter of 2002. WorldCom alone contributed $28.3 billion of defaults in a single quarter. Telecom recoveries averaged about sixteen cents on the dollar. The speculative-grade default rate peaked above 10% in early 2002. That is not equity pain. That is a credit event, in a technology build-out, in the year the essay says had no debt.

The essay mentions telecom zero times. Fiber, zero. WorldCom, zero. The omission is not an accident; it is load-bearing. The telecom precedent comes with an ending the thesis cannot use. Bondholders lost. Equity was wiped out. The banking system shrugged. The 2001 recession lasted eight months and was among the mildest on record. And the fiber - most of it dark when the builders went bankrupt - was eventually lit, and carried the next twenty years of the internet.

The honest bear case for AI is 2001. The essay chose 2008 because 2001 does not get restacked.

IV. The Loan Book That Is a Lease Book

Look past the analogy and read the contracts for what they are. A frontier lab signs an agreement to buy compute over several years. The provider records the unearned portion as a remaining performance obligation - RPO - which is a disclosure, not an asset. Nothing is owed until the service is delivered. The provider delivers compute monthly and bills monthly. If the customer stops paying, the provider stops delivering.

The essay strips this down and declares it a loan: the provider advances capital in the form of a building full of chips, the data center is collateral, and the take-or-pay rate is debt service. But a loan is defined by what happens when the borrower stops paying, and here what happens is the opposite of a loan. A lender who has advanced cash has lost the cash. A provider whose tenant leaves still owns the chips, the building, the power contract and the cooling - and can sell the compute to someone else. The real exposure is not the RPO. It is the capital spent ahead of delivery, and whether that capital can earn elsewhere.

That is a question with an answer, and the answer is a price. If AI compute were a loan book backed by collateral that only one borrower values, then its price would be falling and old hardware would be unrentable. Instead, CoreWeave has said that since June 30, 2026 it has continued to contract new capacity at higher prices. On its first-quarter call, management said 2026 capacity was largely sold out and that pricing was rising across its Ampere, Hopper and Blackwell fleets. Ampere is the A100, a chip launched in 2020. CoreWeave executives have described renewing an old A100 contract at roughly 95% of its original value. Collateral that rents for nearly the same price at age five is not subprime collateral. Try finding a 2006 subdivision whose houses rented for more in 2011.

The essay's accounting is wrong in a second way. It warns that if the tenants default, the backlog turns into non-cash impairments. RPO is not recognized on the balance sheet. There is nothing there to impair. What gets tested for impairment is the property and equipment, and only when its future cash flows cannot recover its carrying value - a test that is hard to fail while capacity is sold out and prices are rising. The essay renamed revenue backlog "lease receivables," renamed capex "origination volume," and then expressed alarm at the size of the loan book it had just invented.

Leases in form; leases in substance. Nobody on Wall Street mistook a loan book for revenue. The essay mistook revenue for a loan book, then congratulated itself on the discovery.

V. The Borrower With an Income

The essay casts OpenAI as this cycle's subprime borrower: a company that can roll its obligations but never pay them down, because it has no income of its own.

OpenAI said in March that it was generating $2 billion of revenue a month - a $24 billion annualized pace. In October the Financial Times reported that its annualized revenue had reached roughly $50 billion at the end of September. The FT also reported that this was about $20 billion short of OpenAI's own projection, and that is a real miss. But a company that roughly doubled its revenue run-rate in six months is many things. "No income" is not one of them. Neither is a company whose growth rate is under attack.

The essay's step-up arithmetic is the part most likely to be screenshotted, so it deserves the closest look. OpenAI's round-over-round valuation step-ups, the essay notes, have fallen from about 1.9x to an implied 1.23x at a public listing - and for a company funded this way, it argues, the markup itself is what pays the bills. It is not. The cash pays the bills. A round priced flat to the last one delivers exactly the same dollars as a round priced at a markup; the markup only decides how much of the company the new money buys. Step-ups compress for every company as it grows, because private marks converge on public prices. Uber's last private valuation was about $76 billion; its IPO priced at about $82 billion, a step-up of roughly 1.08x. Uber is now profitable. A falling step-up sequence is not the signature of a Ponzi. It is the signature of a late-stage company.

Now do the arithmetic the essay is hiding from itself. In Section V it reports that OpenAI's most recent round raised roughly $122 billion of fresh capital. In Section VI it puts OpenAI's cumulative cash burn at about $115 billion through 2029. By the essay's own two numbers, one funding round pre-funded the borrower's entire projected burn through 2029, with seven billion dollars to spare. Four sections later the essay worries that an IPO must somehow pay off that same burn.

To be fair - fairer than the essay is to anyone - the $115 billion figure was stale. It dates from September 2025, and later reporting put OpenAI's projected burn closer to $218 billion for 2026 through 2029. That is a genuinely large number and a genuine risk. But the error is instructive. The essay was not wrong for being too bearish. It was wrong because it never checked whether its own numbers fit inside the same essay.

The essay describes that round as three insiders handing one dollar around a circle and marking it up on every lap. It is a strong image with a bad count. Amazon, Nvidia and SoftBank did supply most of the money, and vendor financing deserves scrutiny. But the round also included funds such as a16z and D. E. Shaw, and more than $3 billion from individual investors. Vendor financing, meanwhile, has a famous precedent: Lucent and Nortel lending to their own customers in 1999 and 2000. Which is to say, again, telecom.

Then the headwinds. The essay says Chinese open-weight models have taken over 60% of OpenRouter tokens, repricing the commodity middle of inference toward zero. The share figure is real; OpenRouter's own data showed 61% in February. But OpenRouter is a routing marketplace whose users skew heavily toward price-sensitive developers, and token share is not revenue share. On Vercel's AI gateway in June, open-weight models handled about 29% of tokens and took roughly one dollar in twenty of spend. In fairness to the essay, that gap is closing: by September, open-weight models were 71.5% of Vercel's tokens and 23% of its spend, a real trend and the essay's best point in this section. But 71.5% of the tokens for 23% of the dollars still says token share overstates revenue share about threefold. Measuring the inference market by OpenRouter share is like measuring the car market by Craigslist listings.

Meanwhile, at Google I/O in May, Sundar Pichai said Google was processing more than 3.2 quadrillion tokens a month, up roughly sevenfold from about 480 trillion a year earlier. The essay's other headwind - token efficiency, cheaper models, trimmed "thinking" - is the Jevons paradox under a new name: make a thing cheaper and people use far more of it. The essay mentions Jevons zero times.

VI. The Lender's Backlog, Graded by the Essay Itself

Accept the essay's headline: roughly $2.1 trillion of contracted backlog across the four big platforms, about half of it owed by OpenAI and Anthropic. Then apply the essay's own credit grades.

The essay rates Anthropic as well insulated: roughly 80% enterprise revenue, $1.70 of revenue for every dollar of compute, and a TPU financing in which Google guarantees lease shortfalls and Broadcom guarantees the residual value of the silicon. The essay itself concludes that those guarantees lift Anthropic's credit to investment grade in effect. So half of the supposedly subprime half is, by the author's own grading, wrapped by investment-grade co-signers. That leaves OpenAI's share - by the essay's count, something like a quarter of the total, not half.

And the insulated borrower is not standing still. Anthropic said its run-rate revenue passed $30 billion in April, up from about $9 billion at the end of 2025. It was about $47 billion when the company announced its Series H in late May. Bloomberg reported in August that it had passed $65 billion. It added roughly $8 billion of run-rate in all of 2025 and more than $50 billion in the first seven months of 2026. By the essay's own favorite metric, this borrower is not decelerating. It is accelerating, at a scale with few precedents in corporate history.

As of two days ago, we no longer have to rely on run-rates. Anthropic made its S-1 public on October 26, with audited numbers. Revenue was $386 million in 2024, $4.6 billion in 2025, and $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026 alone. Second-quarter revenue was $11.6 billion, up from $787 million a year earlier, and the quarter produced GAAP operating income of $1.0 billion, an 8% operating margin. At June 30 the company held $112.4 billion of cash and another $19.3 billion of short-term investments. The borrower with no income has an operating income.

The filing also explains the number that will be screenshotted next: a first-half net loss of $80.7 billion. Of that, $72.4 billion is a non-cash change in the fair value of Anthropic's own convertible notes and related instruments, driven, the S-1 says, by an increase in the company's enterprise valuation. The essay told us that for a frontier lab, the markup is what pays the bills. In the only audited filing we have, the markup is the loss.

The S-1 is not all good news for this rebuttal, and it should not be read that way. Since the start of 2026, Anthropic has signed roughly $417 billion of new minimum payment obligations - compute and data center leases and cloud purchase commitments, some running twenty years - and it estimates another $125-150 billion for TPU capacity delivered between 2028 and 2031. Its commitments to Google (at least $114.5 billion), Amazon (at least $110 billion) and Microsoft (at least $44.9 billion) are owed regardless of actual usage. Some data center leases are backed by partners' guarantees, but its largest equipment lease - about $125 billion of rent over five years - is guaranteed by Anthropic itself, secured by the chips, with cross-default provisions. Against all of that, its customers' contracted backlog is just $10.8 billion; most of its revenue is pay-as-you-go. By the S-1, the essay was too generous in calling Anthropic insulated, and too stingy in assuming a frontier lab cannot earn money. The risk the filing actually shows is a level: hundreds of billions of fixed commitments against revenue that is enormous, profitable at the operating line, and not contracted.

The filing also shows where the essay is right about the neoclouds. Anthropic's GPU agreements with Lambda and Nscale accrue fees only as capacity is delivered and accepted, and can end if the provider fails to deliver or, in Lambda's case, to secure its facility; its compute agreements with SpaceX's AI unit can be cancelled on 90 days' notice once their initial terms end in early 2027. That is protection for the lab and risk for the provider. If anyone in this chain is lending against a tenant's promise, it is the neocloud - and the S-1 says so in the fine print.

Microsoft's book tells the same story from the lender's side. Its commercial RPO reached a record $678 billion at the end of June, and CFO Amy Hood said it rose 25% excluding OpenAI. The non-OpenAI part of the supposed loan book grew by a quarter in a year.

The essay's account of the April 27 amendment between Microsoft and OpenAI is the section that most rewards a click through to the source. Under the new terms, Microsoft stopped paying OpenAI a share of revenue on OpenAI products it resells; OpenAI continues to pay Microsoft, now subject to a cap; Microsoft gave up exclusivity and remains OpenAI's primary cloud. The essay calls this Microsoft walking away from OpenAI's bills. Microsoft never paid OpenAI's bills. It sent them.

Then the essay does something remarkable. In Section VI it cites Microsoft's commitments of more than $60 billion to neocloud providers as proof that Microsoft distrusts OpenAI - a lender cutting its exposure to a borrower it no longer trusts. In Section IX it takes the same contracts, traces them through to OpenAI, and declares CoreWeave's book roughly two-thirds OpenAI. Pick one. If Microsoft is the tenant, the neocloud's counterparty is one of only two American companies S&P rates AAA. If Microsoft is merely a pipe passing OpenAI's demand through, then Microsoft sits between OpenAI and the neocloud's lenders. In either reading, the "look-through" exposure looks straight through the strongest balance sheet in corporate America and declares it transparent.

The essay calls Anthropic's guarantee structure a "monoline wrap," as though invoking 2008 were the same as analyzing it. The monolines of 2008 - Ambac, MBIA - were thinly capitalized insurers that had guaranteed far more than they could ever pay. A monoline was a promise without a balance sheet. Google is a balance sheet with a promise.

VII. The Flip Already Happened

Start with what the essay gets right. Aggregate hyperscaler capex is converging on 100% of operating cash flow in 2026. Alphabet reported negative free cash flow of about $5.9 billion in the second quarter, reportedly its first negative quarter ever. Meta's second-quarter free cash flow fell to under $1 billion from $8.6 billion a year earlier. Morgan Stanley forecast about $570 billion of AI-linked bond issuance this year. All real.

But describing this as a fortress being spent down is a dramatic way of describing corporate finance. Companies rated in the AA range borrowing in the investment-grade market to build assets with sold-out demand is what investment-grade markets are for. American utilities have spent more than their operating cash flow on capital projects for most of a century. As for the essay's claim that hyperscaler leverage now stands at about 1.8 turns, higher than the entire energy sector: I could not find a source for it. The Morgan Stanley data I could find, cited by Man Group in April, said hyperscaler leverage remained lower than most other non-financial sectors at the end of 2025.

Now the game theory, which is the essay's real engine. The capex race, it argues, is a Nash equilibrium sustained by applause: each CFO spends because the market rewards spending, and the moment the market instead punishes spending and rewards restraint, the equilibrium flips, every CFO follows the first one to cut, and the arms race ends. It is a clean, testable prediction. It has been tested.

On January 29, 2026, Microsoft fell about 12% intraday, erasing more than $440 billion of market value - its steepest drop since the pandemic - on investor fatigue with "spend now, profit later." On April 30, Meta fell about 9% after raising its 2026 capex guidance. On July 22, Alphabet slipped after hours when it raised 2026 capex guidance to $195-205 billion. On July 29, Meta fell after hours again when it lifted guidance to $130-145 billion.

The payoff matrix flipped. Spending was punished, publicly and repeatedly. And nobody cut. They raised. Combined 2026 capex guidance for the big four now sits around $720-745 billion. The essay's model predicts that a punished spender blinks; the data show punished spenders spending more. Either every CFO in the industry is irrational in the same direction at the same time, or the spending is anchored to something other than applause - something like a 1.4% vacancy rate and a sold-out order book. The equilibrium is not anchored to belief. It is anchored to backlog.

The essay reaches for Keynes's beauty contest, and for a Morgan Stanley line likening the jump in capex estimates to an auction. The beauty contest is a description of stock pickers guessing what other stock pickers will like. It is a poor description of a CFO whose spending is rationed by the physical world. Hyperscaler capex is not bid up at auction; it is capped by how many gas turbines, transformers, grid interconnections and leading-edge wafers exist, and the queues for several of those run years. When the binding constraint on your spending is your place in line for electrical equipment, you are not spending on what you think the market will reward. You are spending on what you can get.

The essay's star witness for the coming flip is a note from the head of Goldman Sachs's Delta One desk, predicting that the first hyperscaler to signal slower spending will be rewarded, and will hurt chip stocks. It is a reasonable trading call. It is also a trading call, from a desk whose job is to have views on the next few weeks of flows, and it comes with a clean test: some hyperscaler has to signal slower spending first. Ten months into 2026, none has.

The essay's recursion argument - that capex is the demand, and the labs' revenue is hyperscaler spending recycled - runs into the essay's own description of Anthropic as roughly 80% enterprise revenue. Enterprises paying a run-rate north of $65 billion for Claude are not hyperscaler capex in a trench coat.

Finally, the essay's own capex series. By its numbers, capex growth peaked at about 81% in 2024 and has been decelerating since: 77% in 2025, an estimated 52% in 2026. The second derivative rolled over, by the essay's own account, in 2025. Borrowed time began well over a year ago. It is still being borrowed.

VIII. Nobody Blinked

The essay predicts the first hyperscaler to cut will be Meta: dual-class control, a founder who has run the efficiency playbook before, the weakest direct monetization of AI capex, and a CEO who is, in the essay's reading, only waiting for investors to signal that stopping is safe. Investors signaled in April, with a 9% drop. They signaled again in July. He raised the bottom of his guidance range.

Google, the essay says, will not blink, and would benefit if rivals retreat. Correct on the first half: Google raised. Amazon, it says, may have its hand forced by negative free cash flow. Amazon kept building.

Oracle is where the essay deserves its due, and gets it here. Oracle is the weakest large credit in the complex, its backlog is heavily concentrated, and the essay was right to say its stress would show up in the credit market. It has. S&P cut Oracle to BBB-, one notch above junk, in July; its five-year credit default swaps traded around 200 basis points, the widest in roughly eighteen years; and its stock had already been halved from its September 2025 high by the end of January.

But notice what Oracle does to the essay's title. Since late 2025, Oracle's five-year CDS has been the most popular single hedge against an AI bust; Bloomberg reported in November that trading in it had exploded as banks and investors used it as a proxy for the whole build-out. The number nobody watches is quoted on every credit desk's screen every day. The market is not blind to AI credit risk. It built a liquid market to trade it.

IX. The Blast Radius Has a Fuse Box

The essay's most dramatic claim is that OpenAI is the correlation of one: the single variable on which every supplier depends, hidden inside the appearance of diversification, exactly as national home prices were hidden inside 2008's senior tranches.

In 2008 the correlation was hidden. It sat inside AAA-rated tranches whose models assumed thousands of geographically dispersed mortgages would not default together, and the rating, not the investor, did the thinking. Here the concentration is printed on page one. CoreWeave named its customers in the disclosure for its DDTL 5.0 facility - the essay cites that very disclosure. Microsoft discussed OpenAI's share of its backlog on its earnings call. Oracle's concentration is the stated basis for its downgrade. A correlation the issuer discloses, the rating agencies cite and the CDS market trades is not a hidden correlation. It is a priced one.

And 2008's senior tranches were not lethal merely because the mortgages beneath them were correlated. They were lethal because the risk had been re-tranched - mezzanine slices of subprime pools pooled again into CDOs, and slices of those into CDOs of CDOs - until a modest rise in correlated losses could wipe out paper rated AAA. Then that AAA paper was held, with borrowed money, by the very institutions that were supposed to absorb shocks. There is no CDO-squared of data center leases. The senior debt in these deals sits above real equity, not above a thinner slice of someone else's mezzanine.

Then size. The essay's evidence that this correlated exposure is being securitized and sold into the wider credit market is DDTL 5.0: $3.1 billion. Subprime mortgages outstanding in 2007 were about $1.3 trillion, roughly four hundred times larger. The paper has entered the credit market the way a raindrop enters the Atlantic.

Most importantly, ownership. Who holds AI debt? Investment-grade bond funds, insurers, and private credit funds whose capital is locked up for years. Long-dated money holding long-dated assets. There is no overnight repo funding these positions, no demand deposits, no asset-backed commercial paper conduits rolling every thirty days. Without short-term funding, there is nothing to run. The transmission line that turned subprime losses into 2008 was the funding structure of the lenders, and the essay never once examines who funds the lenders. Losses without a run are a bad year. Losses with a run are 2008.

As for the claim that chip stocks fell because OpenAI signaled it might delay its IPO: in the same week, coverage also cited soaring memory costs and hot inflation data, and at least one market recap said outright that it was unclear whether the moves were tied to OpenAI at all. Attribution by headline is not a transmission mechanism.

X. The Refinance of First Resort

The essay frames OpenAI's eventual IPO as the refinance of last resort, and the S-1 as a trap: the document that unlocks public capital will also put the losses, the concentration and the hundreds of billions of take-or-pay obligations in front of every reader. OpenAI, it says, needs public money but cannot survive public scrutiny.

OpenAI's revenue, losses and burn projections have been reported in detail, roughly quarterly, by The Information and others for two years. The essay itself is built almost entirely from those reports. The S-1 will be the first time the numbers are audited. It will not be the first time anyone reads them.

To be fair to the trap, it has sprung before. WeWork's 2019 S-1 sank its IPO, because it revealed a property lessee with a technology valuation. But the more common outcome for a heavily loss-making company with fast-growing revenue is the other one. Uber went public the same year with an S-1 showing an operating loss of about $3 billion, and the deal got done. Investors read losses all the time. What they price is whether the revenue is real, and a run-rate that doubled in six months from customers who chose to pay is about as real as revenue gets.

OpenAI confidentially submitted a draft S-1 in June. Anthropic did the same on June 1, and on October 26 it made its S-1 public - audited losses, risk factors, take-or-pay schedules and all. The scrutiny the essay said a frontier lab could not survive turned out to include a quarter of operating profit. These are not companies hiding from the public market; they are companies lining up for it. The implied 1.23x step-up from $852 billion to a $1 trillion-plus listing is the lowest in the sequence because an IPO step-up is normally the lowest in the sequence. See Uber, above.

The essay then sets a puzzle for itself: the IPO must clear at a price the public will pay and raise enough to cover the roughly $115 billion of cumulative burn, and it cannot do both. No IPO pays off future burn. An IPO raises a tranche of capital and gives the company a currency. And by the essay's own figures, the private round five sections earlier had already raised more than the burn it says the IPO must cover. The trap is real only inside the essay, which is where it was built.

XI. How It Doesn't Break

The essay's six-step sequence deserves to be read slowly. Step one: the IPO prices below the required mark. Step two: OpenAI pulls back on compute commitments, which the essay treats as a covenant breach by the provider whose debt rests on those commitments. That confuses the borrower with the lender. A take-or-pay contract obliges the customer to pay whether or not it takes delivery; that is what take-or-pay means. A customer that walks away is in breach of its own contract, and the provider holds a claim against it. The provider's lenders are protected by the very structure the essay describes as fragile.

The essay supplies a more basic tell itself. At the end of Section VII it predicts that what follows the flip is not a clean default but a negotiation: volumes cut, schedules stretched, contracts restructured. In 2008, that was exactly what could not happen. Mortgages had been sold into securitization trusts with thousands of scattered investors, servicers often lacked the authority or the incentive to modify them, and research by Tomasz Piskorski, Amit Seru and Vikrant Vig found delinquent securitized loans were foreclosed more often than comparable loans banks kept - a finding others later argued was smaller than first estimated, though not that it was absent. Foreclosure became the default outcome because renegotiation was structurally blocked. A compute contract between OpenAI and Microsoft is a bilateral agreement between two of the most sophisticated counterparties on earth, each with every reason to keep the other alive. The essay's own forecast - a renegotiation among a handful of parties - is the opposite of the 2008 mechanism. It is how commercial disputes end.

Step three - the neoclouds - is where the essay is closest to right, and where a rebuttal should concede. CoreWeave and its peers are levered spread businesses with concentrated customers and speculative-grade ratings. If losses come, they will land there first. That is what happened to the competitive local exchange carriers in 2001. It is a real risk to real bondholders and shareholders.

Step four - the credit freeze across the complex - requires runnable funding, which, as above, is absent. Step five - equities fall - is a thing equities do. Step six - the strong buy stranded data centers for pennies - requires stranded data centers, in a market where 1.4% of capacity is vacant.

And then the concession that undoes the whole structure: the author admits to having no idea when any of this happens. A mechanism with no timing, triggered by a deceleration that is mathematically certain to occur on every S-curve ever drawn, is not a forecast. It is a horoscope that has read a calculus textbook. The essay closed with the warning that the clock is ticking. It was published on July 2. In the 118 days since, capex guidance went up, data center vacancy went down, GPU rental prices went up, Anthropic's run-rate went up by tens of billions and its S-1 showed an operating profit, and OpenAI's run-rate roughly doubled from March. The clock is ticking. So is every clock.

XII. The Strongest Case Against This Essay

Grant the bears their strongest case, because there is one. OpenAI projects losses that run into the hundreds of billions before it turns cash-flow positive, and it is running behind its own revenue plan. SoftBank took a $40 billion unsecured bridge loan, due in March 2027, to fund its OpenAI commitments; it has reportedly been refinancing part of it since, but it remains the single most levered node in the system. Anthropic's own S-1 shows more than $400 billion of new fixed compute commitments signed this year, against $10.8 billion of contracted customer backlog. Oracle is one notch from junk with a concentrated book. The neoclouds are spread trades with thin equity. Hyperscaler free cash flow is near zero. Nvidia and Amazon invest in OpenAI, and OpenAI buys from Nvidia and Amazon. Any of these could go badly, and some of them probably will.

Write that essay honestly and it says something like this: AI equities are expensive; lenders to the weakest names may lose money; Oracle may lose its investment-grade rating; SoftBank may have a very bad 2027; and if the frontier labs miss their plans, the neoclouds will be the first to feel it. That is a respectable essay. It is about twelve hundred words long. It does not need 2008, a Nash equilibrium, a monoline, Keynes's beauty contest, an invented loan book or calculus.

It is also not new to this publication. Its archive lists "Peak Cheap: The AI Boom Isn't 2000, It's 2008" on June 7, "The Second Derivative" on July 2, and "The Teaser Period: Why the AI Boom is Hitting a Reset Wall" on August 20. Three versions of one thesis in eleven weeks. If we are measuring second derivatives, the one that has rolled over is novelty.

The essay and I agree on the risks. We disagree about which year they rhyme with.

XIII. The Zeroth Derivative

Three mistakes, layered, make "The Second Derivative." It remembers 2008 without its funding structure, which was the only part that made it 2008. It forgets the one technology build-out that was actually financed with debt, because that one ended in a mild recession and a lot of useful fiber. And it renames ordinary deceleration as a secret variable, so that a fact about every growth story in history can be sold as a discovery about this one.

Solvency is a level. Cash on hand. Debt outstanding. Interest coverage. Who funds the lenders, and how quickly they can leave. 2008 broke on levels: thirty-to-one leverage, overnight funding, and a trillion dollars of bad loans inside a system that could not absorb a fraction of that in losses without a run. If the AI build-out breaks, it will break on levels too - OpenAI's cash against its commitments, Oracle's debt against its earnings, SoftBank's bridge loan against its stake. Those are numbers everyone watches. They are on Bloomberg every morning.

The essay gets 2008 backwards and leaves out the half of 2000 that matters. And it is built, with real craft, to sound right to anyone who does not check.

The Third Derivative.
